# Part II: Teaching Direction, Pedagogy & Clarification Checklist

*Consolidated from `Music Project - Teaching Direction Brainstorm.md` (5 Oct 2026), `Music Workshop - Clarification Checklist.md` (5 Oct 2026), and `Music Workshop - Session 1 - Participant Sounds and Critical Listening.txt` (28 Sep 2026).*

## 2.1 Core Vision & Audience

### The Project in One Sentence
This is a listening-based music workshop in which participants learn to hear musical structure, performance choices, words, and social context, then use what they hear to respond and create with others.

### Universal Objective
Participants become more independent listeners and makers. They can describe what they hear, question an automatic interpretation, connect a musical detail to the larger passage, and make a musical or poetic choice in response to another person.

The project is not only about learning musical technique. Technique is used to make listening, interpretation, and shared creation possible.

### Who the Workshop Serves
The initial audience is Persian-speaking people approximately **15–30**, primarily living in Iran, with different levels of experience:
1. **Vocal/Listening Entry:** People who can sing or participate vocally (or through listening/movement) but want to understand what they hear.
2. **Practicing Musicians:** People who have practiced music and want to understand more clearly what they are playing.
3. **Makers/Adapters:** People who have learned music and want to create their own musical or poetic material.

These groups can work together if every activity has an accessible entry point and a deeper optional challenge. The public promise remains beginner-friendly: **no advanced theory or reading ability is required**. Teaching is in Persian, with selected English musical terms introduced after practical experience.

### Relation Between Poetry and Music
The combination of poetry and music is an individual and collective practice. Participants first hear how words and music interact in a chosen song, then test their own wording, rhythm, emphasis, and melodic choices.

The workshop does not assume that a word has one fixed musical emotion. Participants distinguish:
- **What they can hear in the sound** (audible observation).
- **What the words say or suggest** (lyrical/semantic meaning).
- **What they personally imagine** (personal interpretation/association).
- **What the historical or social context documents** (verified context).

## 2.2 Pedagogical References (Bernstein, Kodály, Dalcroze, Adorno)

### 1. Leonard Bernstein — Primary Teaching Reference
Leonard Bernstein's *Young People's Concerts* are the main reference for the workshop's presentation style and teaching practice:
- Start with a real musical example.
- Ask a concrete question before explaining the term.
- Demonstrate the idea by singing, playing, or comparing excerpts.
- Invite the audience to answer or perform.
- Return to the music and test the idea by listening again.
- Move from a small musical idea to a larger phrase or passage.

Bernstein is a model for explanation and participation, not a complete method to copy. Keep explanations short and use examples suited to this group; do not treat one musical style as a standard of superiority.
- [**Young People's Concerts Overview**](https://www.leonardbernstein.com/about/educator/young-peoples-concerts)
- [**“What Is a Melody?”**](https://leonardbernstein.com/lectures/television-scripts/young-peoples-concerts/what-is-melody) — First study reference; connects small musical ideas into phrases and larger passages and examines repetition with variation.
- [**“What Makes Music Symphonic?”**](https://leonardbernstein.com/lectures/television-scripts/young-peoples-concerts/what-makes-music-symphonic) — Demonstrates how a recognizable idea develops through audible changes.
- [**“What Does Music Mean?”**](https://leonardbernstein.com/lectures/television-scripts/young-peoples-concerts/what-does-music-mean) — Distinguishes musical relationships from an attached narrative and demonstrates changes in expressive effect. (His position is an argument to consider, not a rule that words require a musical change.)

### 2. Kodály & Dalcroze — Practical Access & Embodied Sequencing
- **Kodály-inspired work** ([OAKE — The Kodály Concept](https://www.oake.org/the-kodaly-concept/)): Contributes singing, echo imitation, ear development, and gradual sequencing. Neutral syllables (`la`) are sufficient; movable-do solfège or rhythm syllables can be introduced when useful and within the facilitator’s teaching competence.
- **Dalcroze-inspired work** ([Dalcroze Society of America](https://www.dalcrozeusa.org/about/what-is-dalcroze/)): Contributes purposeful movement, listening, and improvisation to explore timing, pulse, phrase shape, and expression.

### 3. Theodor W. Adorno — Critical Listening & Structural Awareness
Our classroom activities are our own adaptation informed by Adorno, not exercises prescribed by Adorno. We do not adopt his categorical judgments about popular music as the class's conclusion.
- **Practical Critical Cycle:**
  `Hear → describe → question an assumption → compare by changing something → give a reason → revise or retain a judgment.`
- **Three Critical Threads:**
  1. **Familiarity:** Am I noticing the sound or mainly recognizing its source? Where do I encounter it—in work, home, public space, or media?
  2. **Relationships (Detail & Whole):** What does this part do alone and within the passage? What changes when it stops or moves?
  3. **Independent Judgment:** What supports my preference? May I disagree with the teacher or keep my first opinion after comparison?
- **Documented Source Basis & Limits:**
  - **Dirk Braunstein (ed.), *Die Frankfurter Seminare Theodor W. Adornos, Band 3: Sommersemester 1961–Wintersemester 1963/64* (De Gruyter, 2021)**, section “Musiksoziologie: Vorlesung mit anschließenden Besprechungen,” printed pp. 197–260 (PDF pp. 207–270). Local file: `C:/Users/Adel/Downloads/die-frankfurter-seminare-theodor-w-adornos-band-3-sommersemester-1961-wintersemester-1963-64-9783110722802-9783110707342-9783111110288_compress.pdf`.
    - *Printed p. 213 / PDF p. 223 (Klaus Döll's report, 12 Dec 1961):* Adorno is reported as explaining that chamber musicians must follow other parts as the musical thread passes between instruments; structural listening can develop from this practice. This strengthens the workshop's listening-dependent exchanges.
    - *Printed p. 214 / PDF p. 224 (same report):* Acknowledges limits to theoretical explanation. Our application is to permit uncertainty and test interpretations instead of forcing a single explanation.
    - *Printed p. 244 / PDF p. 254 (Werner Matthes's report, 30 Jan 1962):* Distinguishes Adorno's position from Paul Engel's and records Adorno questioning a direct correspondence between general and musical education. Do not attribute Engel's claims to Adorno or rank learners by schooling.
  - **Theodor W. Adorno, with George Simpson, [“On Popular Music” (1941)](https://260mc.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/10/adorno-on-popular-music.pdf):** Relevant for recognition, standardization, and detail/whole relationships.
  - **Alexandra Kertz-Welzel, [“The Pied Piper of Hamelin: Adorno on Music Education” (2005)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1321103X050250010301):** Supports emphasis on critical, self-determined students and questioning ideological uses of music education.
  - **Theodor W. Adorno, [“Analytical Study of the NBC Music Appreciation Hour” (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mq/78.2.325):** *The Musical Quarterly* 78(2), pp. 325–377.

## 3.1 Course Overview, Learning Outcomes & Session Rhythm

- **Title:** *Hearing Each Other Through Music* — a continuing listening-based workshop; this section covers the first four sessions.
- **Format:** Current intake online via Google Meet. Registration covers four initial sessions. Duration, capacity and schedule remain pending; older weekly/75-minute/6–8-person figures are planning assumptions, not confirmed booking details.
- **Status of plans below:** Earlier detailed designs are reference drafts. Use the revised Persian proposal and the current decisions above to prepare the executable first-session plan.
- **Guiding Questions:**
  - *Overarching:* How can we listen to another person and contribute to music we make together?
  - *Phrase & Word Focus:* How does a musical phrase become recognizable, and what happens when its words or delivery change? When words change their meaning, what musical choices can express, complicate, or leave that change open?
- **Learning Sequence:**
  - *Earlier proposed first-block arc (reference):* `Listen → feel → imitate → recognize → vary → connect → add words → exchange and revise → apply elsewhere.`
  - *Critical inquiry cycle:* `Hear → describe → imitate or try → compare → question → revise or retain a judgment.`
- **Learning Outcomes:** By the end of the four sessions, participants should be able to:
  1. Recognize a short phrase and describe its return or change.
  2. Explore its pulse, rhythm, and melodic shape through voice, movement, or another accessible response.
  3. Make one simple variation and connect phrases into a passage.
  4. Relate an audible musical feature to an interpretation of the words, separating observation from interpretation.
  5. Contribute to Persian wording and delivery that fit the musical material.
  6. Explain how hearing a partner influenced a revision.
  7. Apply the same listening questions to another short song excerpt with less guidance.
- **Recurring Listening Questions:**
  - Where does an idea begin and end?
  - What returns, and what changes?
  - Where does the phrase feel complete or unfinished?
  - How do the phrases connect?
  - What happens when the same musical shape carries different words?
  - What does the performer emphasize, repeat, delay, or leave open?
  - What in the sound supports your interpretation?
  - What did you hear from someone else that changed your response?
- **Flexible 75-Minute Session Rhythm:**
  - `00–05 min (5m)`: Arrival and recall
  - `05–15 min (10m)`: Listening and context (or gathering participant sounds)
  - `15–30 min (15m)`: Demonstration and imitation
  - `30–35 min (5m)`: Break
  - `35–60 min (25m)`: Exploration, role exchange, and collaboration
  - `60–70 min (10m)`: Sharing, comparison, and reflection
  - `70–75 min (5m)`: Close and choosing a small between-session practice

### Track A (Primary Plan): Recognize a Musical Phrase — Bernstein-Style Demonstration, Variation & Persian Words
*(Consolidated from `Music Project - Teaching Direction Brainstorm.md` [Oct 5] & `Music Workshop - Teaching Proposal.txt` [Sep 26])*

- **Central Question:** *How does a musical phrase become recognizable, and what happens when its words or delivery change?*
  - **پرسش محوری جلسه:** یک جملهٔ موسیقایی چطور در گوش ما قابل‌تشخیص می‌شود، و وقتی کلمات یا شیوهٔ بیانِ آن عوض می‌شود چه اتفاقی می‌افتد؟
- **Central Concept:** A recognizable phrase establishes an expectation through repetition; small changes in melody, rhythm, emphasis, or words shift how we hear and respond to it.
- **Starting Musical Material:**
  - **Primary Excerpt:** A short passage (15–30 seconds) from Nina Simone's *“Ain't Got No, I Got Life”* (1968 `'Nuff Said!` studio recording), plus the corresponding short excerpt from *Hair* (1968 Original Broadway Cast Recording) for comparison.
  - **Facilitator's Simplified Model (`Phrase A` & `Variation B`):** A short, comfortable vocal/instrumental phrase derived from or inspired by the excerpt, sung first on a neutral syllable (`la` / `لا`) and then with a short Persian phrase.
- **Evidence of Learning:** By the end of the 75 minutes, participants can:
  1. Identify one repeated phrase and where it begins/ends.
  2. Describe one audible change (in rhythm, pitch ending, emphasis, or pause), separating **what they heard** from **what they interpreted**.
  3. Echo, hum, tap, or direct the simplified musical phrase `A` and one variation `B`.
  4. Make one musical or poetic choice in Persian and explain how listening to another participant/group influenced their decision.

### Track B: Participant Sounds, Imitation & Adorno-Informed Critical Listening
*(From `Music Workshop - Session 1 - Participant Sounds and Critical Listening.txt` [Sep 28])*

- **Central Musical Question:** What happens to a sound when we repeat it and place it beside other sounds?
- **Central Critical Question:** What did I actually hear, what did I assume, and what would make me reconsider?
- **Sequence:** `Notice → contribute → imitate → shape → exchange → combine → compare → revise.`
- **Musical Sources & Materials:**
  - The **first sound** comes from a participant: surroundings, daily routines, remembered sound patterns, or speech (no predetermined melody or Nina excerpt required to start).
  - Essential materials: voices, bodies, chairs, timer, paper/cards. Optional: piano/instrument, phone.
- **Detailed 75-Minute Timeline (Track B):**
  - **0–5 min — Invitation:**
    - Explain: “We will begin with sounds you bring, find ways to repeat and combine them, and listen to what changes.” Allow listening or passing. Demonstrate start, stop, and space signals.
    - Persian opening: «امروز از صداهایی شروع می‌کنیم که شما می‌شنوید. با هم امتحان می‌کنیم چطور آن‌ها را تکرار و ترکیب کنیم و بشنویم چه چیزی تغییر می‌کند.»
  - **5–15 min — Participants Offer Sounds:**
    - Short quiet listening period, then invite current or remembered sounds (imitate, describe, or pass). Do not rate their musical worth.
    - Ask: “What did you notice first? Why might that sound attract your attention?”
  - **15–25 min — Select, Echo, and Shape:**
    - Choose 1–2 contributions together for practical reasons (easy to reproduce, short enough to remember, useful contrast). Confirm adaptations with the contributor.
    - Echo once, then repeat. If irregular, compare original imitation with a regularized version using gesture/movement.
    - Ask: “What did we preserve? What did we change to repeat it? Does the regular version lose anything?”
  - **25–35 min — Listen to Speech:**
    - Invite one short participant-provided Persian phrase. Keep words, then change one feature at a time: emphasis, duration, or vocal pitch contour (hum, speak, or gesture).
    - Ask first for an observation, then an interpretation: “Which sound changed? What did that suggest to you?”
  - **35–40 min — Break**
  - **40–50 min — Establish Roles Through Exchange:**
    - Divide into two groups: one offers the chosen pattern, the other answers after a clear gap. Swap roles.
    - Establish complementary roles (e.g., gentle pulse + short pattern/vocal response; third role optional).
    - Ask: “What do you need to hear before you enter?”
    - *Optional Chamber-Music Handoff (adapted from Adorno Frankfurt Seminar Band 3, p. 213):* Pass the participant-derived pattern from A to B to C; let the previous group's audible ending cue the next entrance. Ask each group to name one feature retained from the preceding version.
  - **50–65 min — Build, Compare, and Revise the Whole:**
    - Arrange a short passage with agreed entrances and ending. Perform once. Remove one part and repeat: what changes in the whole?
    - Restore it, then change one feature (volume, entry order, response gap). Compare versions and choose with reasons.
    - Prompt: «چه چیزی از گروه دیگر شنیدی که باعث شد اجرای خودت را تغییر بدهی؟» / «کدام صدا کمتر شنیده شد؟ چه چیزی در اجرای ما باعثش شد؟ چه تغییری را امتحان کنیم؟»
    - One group directs a revision while the other plays, then swap.
  - **65–72 min — Reflect and Try a New Case:**
    - Consider an unused participant sound: where might it fit and what would change?
    - Reflection frame: “I heard \_\_\_. At first I assumed \_\_\_. After comparison I chose \_\_\_ because \_\_\_.” Ask which facilitator suggestion they accepted or rejected and why.
  - **72–75 min — Close and Continue Listening:**
    - Each participant names one adjustment made because of someone else's sound.
    - Between-session task: Notice one sound, remember/describe/imitate/record it, and bring one observation about repetition and one question.

## 3.3 Session 2 — Side-by-Side Tracks

### Track A: Develop and Connect Ideas (Phrase Variation & Transition)
*(From `Music Workshop - Teaching Proposal.txt` [Sep 26])*

- **Central Concept:** Variation changes a recognizable idea; transition connects ideas.
- **Activities:**
  - Groups learn phrase `A` and its variation `B` (where `B` changes one feature while others remain stable), then exchange roles.
  - Groups choose one feature to preserve, one to alter, and a way to connect the phrases.
  - A sequence such as `A → A → B → A` is a starting model, not a required final form.
  - Participants try connections through a pause, phrase ending, or continued pulse. Each response retains or reacts to something heard in the other group's performance.
- **Evidence of Learning:** Demonstrate a variation, identify a connection, and explain what another person's contribution influenced.

### Track B: When Do We Hear Sound as Music? (Context, Interpretation & Shared Bandari Rhythm)
*(From `Music Workshop - Sessions 1 and 2 - Working Draft.txt` [Sep 28])*

- **Purpose:** Investigate how sounds, musical relationships, context, and expectations shape interpretation. Extend shared listening through a selected Bandari example and practical pattern-making.
- **Core Questions:** Would you call this music? What supports your answer? Does the music itself tell one story? What changes when its words, delivery, or context changes?
- **Sources & Materials to Prepare:**
  - A sustained vocal or instrumental sound.
  - One short excerpt with verified source and context (potentially the same Bandari example used later; if comparing wedding vs. procession/march, verify actual examples rather than assuming identical rhythms).
  - A short teachable pattern from the selected Bandari source.
- **Detailed 75-Minute Timeline (Track B):**
  - **0–5 min — Return to Session 1:** Hear 1–2 observations participants bring; recall how patterns changed when combined.
  - **5–15 min — Sustain a Sound and Open the Question:**
    - Sing or play one held sound. Ask: «آیا این را موسیقی می‌دانید؟ چه چیزی در صدا یا موقعیت باعث می‌شود این‌طور فکر کنید؟»
    - Allow yes, no, and uncertainty. Test by changing duration, silence, repetition, or placement.
  - **15–30 min — Hear, Describe, and Discuss Context:**
    - Play the short excerpt before supplying context. Invite audible observation and personal interpretation.
    - Keep two investigations distinct:
      1. Replay the **same** excerpt with different imagined situations; discuss changes in interpretation.
      2. Compare **different** documented performances or reveal the actual context; identify what is known and what changed in the sound.
  - **30–35 min — Break**
  - **35–50 min — Learn the Selected Bandari Pattern:**
    - Explain the source and limited passage studied. Demonstrate a manageable version and invite imitation (slower model or shorter segment as needed, then reconnect with source). Avoid presenting one pattern as the definition of all Bandari music.
  - **50–65 min — Hold a Role, Hear a Response, Explore Words:**
    - Bridge: *“We heard how a passage can invite different interpretations. Now we will keep a shared pattern and explore how words, delivery, and responses affect what others hear.”*
    - One group maintains the familiar rhythmic role while another offers a short response in clear gaps, then swap.
    - Explore a short Persian phrase over the pattern: keep rhythm stable for one comparison, then test one chosen musical change.
  - **65–75 min — Compare and Reflect:**
    - Repeat two versions. Prompt: «در صدا چه چیزی تغییر کرد؟ در برداشت تو چه چیزی تغییر کرد؟ برای هر کدام چه نشانه‌ای داری؟»
    - End with one observation and one unresolved question.
- **Four Explicit OPEN Design Questions for Session 2 (Track B):**
  1. **Which outcome leads Session 2?** Is success primarily explaining how context affects interpretation, or maintaining one rhythmic role while hearing another? (`Status: OPEN`)
  2. **Why Bandari here?** What particular recording, performer, or practice connects it to the question about musical meaning? What exact pattern and context will we teach? (`Status: OPEN`)
  3. **What exactly does using Kodály mean here?** Echoing, rhythm syllables, singing, or another specific tool? What learning difficulty does it address? (`Status: OPEN`)
  4. **What will participants decide?** Their words, an answering pattern, the arrangement, or the intended expression? (`Status: OPEN`)

## 3.4 Session 3 — Connect Persian Words and Musical Contrast

- **Central Concept:** Verbal meaning and musical expression can change together or independently.
- **Activities:**
  - Return to the familiar melody. Replace neutral syllables with short Persian words and explore:
    `«من ندارم…» ← «پس من چی دارم؟» ← پاسخ انتخابی شرکت‌کنندگان`
  - Compare **changed words over unchanged music** with **changed words plus one musical alteration**.
  - Participants choose an intention—curiosity, insistence, uncertainty, or another reading—and test how listeners receive it. They may leave the question unresolved.
  - Revise wording for natural Persian stress, syllable count, and musical fit. Record which text and musical choices belong to the group's adaptation.
- **Evidence of Learning:** Identify an audible feature, distinguish it from an interpretation, and revise a choice after hearing another person's response.

## 3.5 Session 4 — Create Together and Apply Elsewhere

- **Central Concept:** Musical structure is an expressive and collaborative choice.
- **Activities:**
  - Assemble and refine a short collective Persian adaptation (indicative length: **45–90 seconds**; a shorter coherent piece is acceptable). Participants decide phrase order, wording, variation, transitions, and delivery.
  - Share the piece and explain one choice influenced by another participant.
  - **Transfer:** Hear another short excerpt from a different style or context. With reduced prompting, identify a phrase, repetition or change, and a possible relationship to words. Propose one adaptation and explain the reason.
- **Evidence of Learning:** Make observations grounded in the new excerpt, alongside a collective piece that reflects participants' decisions.

## 3.6 Facilitator Boundaries, Online Adaptation & Preparation

### Facilitator Boundaries
- Keep cultural and historical framing brief so it does not displace listening practice.
- Separate **observation** (what is heard in pitch, timing, loudness, tone color), **interpretation** (personal reading of effect), and **verified cultural/historical context**.
- Let *“What makes this musical?”* remain an open inquiry; do not rank participants' sounds, cultures, or musical tastes.
- Repetition is both a learning aid and something to examine; it is not automatically uncritical or inferior.
- Reproducing pitches accurately is not the only measure of learning; humming, speech, seated movement, drawing, pointing to a moment, or directing the facilitator are all valid ways to demonstrate understanding.

### Online vs. In-Person Adaptation
| Activity | In-Person | Online (Skyroom / Zoom) |
|---|---|---|
| **Join a pulse** | Tap together and listen across the group | Follow the facilitator while muted, then take turns demonstrating |
| **Hear and echo** | Partners exchange phrases in real time | One person offers a phrase, stops, and another answers; allow for latency delay |
| **Add melody / layers** | Hear a partner or small group simultaneously | Practice along with the facilitator while muted, then receive individual feedback |
| **Make a shared piece** | Rehearse a simultaneous group performance | Build a sequential passage with clear turns, or assemble consented short recordings (do not rely on simultaneous ensemble playing over a standard call) |

### Preparation Checklist Before Teaching
1. Confirm one Nina Simone recording (`'Nuff Said!`, May 13, 1968 studio track) and mark exact short excerpts alongside the 1968 Broadway *Hair* tracks.
2. Verify what changes audibly and how it relates to the selected words.
3. Prepare a comfortable simplified phrase `A`, a clear variation `B`, and a transition/connection.
4. Rehearse sung demonstrations; arrange optional instrumental support if useful (no external performer is assumed).
5. Prepare a short Persian text with at least two workable delivery choices.
6. Select 1–2 accessible comparison excerpts with explained lyrics where needed.
7. Try a **10-minute sequence with a beginner** and revise unclear instructions before advertising a paid course.
8. Confirm venue or online setup (test Skyroom/Zoom audio), group size, dates, materials, and fee.

## 5.3 Work System & Workshop Readiness Checklist

### 8-Rule Work System
1. **Open one working page:** Part I of this document holds Now, Next, and Later.
2. **Start physically:** Use the task's first physical action (e.g., opening one link or recording one sentence).
3. **Choose a short block:** Set a 20–30 minute timer if useful; stop or continue deliberately.
4. **Finish visibly:** Check off the task, save its output, and write the next physical action in one sentence.
5. **Park new ideas:** Add one line to Later; return to the current task.
6. **Limit cosmetic revisions:** Keep the working name and basic visual style through the pilot; try up to three full recording takes, then patch specific lines.
7. **Handle overruns deliberately:** If a task runs ~25 minutes past estimate, stop and choose: simplify, ask for help, use contingency, or move the date.
8. **Restart gently:** After a missed session, reopen the last next-action note and move dates forward without doubling the next day's workload.

### Workshop Readiness Gate (Before Advertising a Paid Pilot)
- [ ] A clear beginner learning outcome.
- [ ] A demonstration and a simpler alternative you can lead confidently.
- [ ] A short trial with a beginner and one improvement based on actual feedback.
- [ ] An in-person room or online setup (Skyroom/Zoom) that has been audio-tested.
- [ ] Duration, capacity, fee and costs, registration route, and cancellation/refund arrangements.
- [ ] A public description that matches what is actually taught.

### Pricing Worksheet & Formulas
Define variables in your chosen operating currency:
- $P$: Fee per participant for the first four-session registration block; amount pending. This does not price the entire continuing workshop.
- $n$: Number of paying participants (calculate conservatively, not at full capacity).
- $F$: Fixed costs per course (venue, equipment hire, allocated software/content costs, fixed payment fees).
- $V$: Variable cost per participant.
- $f$: Percentage payment fee expressed as a decimal.
- $H$: All course-related work hours (delivery, preparation, administration, promotion, and allocated episode production).
- $R$: Target payment to yourself per hour.

$$\text{Cash left for your work before tax} = n \times [P \times (1 - f) - V] - F$$

$$\text{Surplus after valuing your time} = n \times [P \times (1 - f) - V] - F - (H \times R)$$

$$\text{Minimum enrolment} = \left\lceil \frac{F + H \times R}{P \times (1 - f) - V} \right\rceil$$
